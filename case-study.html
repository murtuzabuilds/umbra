The first wave of startups got bought

Between 2024 and 2025 the leading AI security startups were bought by platform vendors: Robust Intelligence by Cisco, Protect AI by Palo Alto Networks, Lakera by Check Point, Prompt Security by SentinelOne. That tells me the problem is real. It also means most of these tools now sit inside large security suites, priced for big enterprises and built mainly to block things.

The regulation is arriving in stages

The EU AI Act's transparency duties (Article 50) apply from 2 August 2026. The 2026 Digital Omnibus moved the stand-alone high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027. They were delayed, not dropped, and companies will be asked to show evidence of how they govern AI.

02  What I believe

For the last few years most of the energy has gone into building with AI. Very little has gone into keeping track of it. I think the harder problem now is governing the AI that is already inside every company, and doing it without slowing people down.

IDEA 1

Unapproved AI use tells you what people need.

When someone uses an unapproved tool, it is usually because they have a job to do and nothing approved helps them do it. Umbra treats that as useful information. It groups the activity by team and task and gives the CIO a ranked list of where an approved tool would help most. This is where my digital transformation work comes in.

IDEA 2

Let AI help write the rules, but not enforce them.

Umbra's AI layer, Corona, drafts rules from plain English, spots AI services that are not in the catalog and explains incidents. The decision to block or allow is always made by fixed rules that give the same answer every time. Anything Corona writes is tested against past traffic and approved by a person before it goes live. Rule drafting now runs on a real model, and I measured it: section 07 has the numbers.

IDEA 3

Treat AI agents like employees with access.

Agents now call other agents that call tools, sometimes three steps deep, and the chain can end up with no single owner. Umbra gives each agent a named owner and a written list of what it is allowed to do, checks every call against it, and estimates how much data someone could reach if the agent were compromised.

04  Principles

Six decisions I made early and kept.

01

See first, then govern

A company cannot manage AI use it cannot see, so discovery comes first. It works from logs the company already has, with nothing to install.

02

Predictable enforcement

Blocking and masking are done by fixed rules and tested detectors. The same input always gets the same decision, with a record an auditor can follow. No AI model makes that call.

03

Guide people before blocking them

Where it is safe, Umbra masks the sensitive part and lets the request through, or suggests an approved tool. Hard blocks are kept for passwords, keys and regulated data.

04

Every agent has an owner

No agent runs without a named person responsible for it and a clear list of what it may do. More freedom is given deliberately, as trust builds.

05

Evidence as a side effect

Every decision Umbra makes is already logged, so audit evidence can be exported at any time instead of pulled together by hand before an audit.

06

Measure adoption

The main metric is the share of AI work done in approved tools. It only goes up when people are both protected and well served, which is the outcome both security and the business want.

05  The product

A console for the whole picture.

Everything below works in the interactive demo (sample data), which runs on a tested engine (70 tests). The demo company is Kestrel Mutual, a made-up insurer with 2,400 staff and 30 days of generated activity.

Overview

How much of your AI use you can actually see

The Overview starts with the share of AI activity Umbra can see and classify. Next to it are the share of work on approved tools, how much sensitive data was contained, how many incidents are open, and a short weekly summary from Corona (AI assistant) with the most useful next steps. In the middle is Umbra at the centre: every AI tool at Kestrel Mutual on orbit, and each ring around a tool is a control that applies to it. Approved tools sit inside all six rings, unapproved ones further out, high-risk ones near the edge.

AI map

Every AI service on one map

Distance from the centre shows risk, size shows usage, and colour shows whether it is approved. Risk is computed from facts a buyer can verify: whether the vendor trains on inputs, how long it keeps them, which attestations it holds and where it processes data. Corona can also recognise AI services that are not in the catalog, just from the shape of the traffic.

Inspect

Each prompt checked, with the reasons shown

The detectors check that a match is real before flagging it: card numbers must pass the Luhn check, bank account numbers the IBAN checksum, and US social security numbers the issuing rules. Each decision shows what was actually sent and which rules applied.

  • Secrets, health data, personal and financial data, source code, codenames, confidentiality markers
  • Stable placeholders, so a redacted prompt still makes sense to the model
Agents

Agents with owners and limits

The graph shows which people use which agents, and which agents call other agents and tools. Calls outside an agent's allowed list show in dashed red. It also shows indirect access: in the demo, the broker assistant can reach financial data it was never given, because it is allowed to call an agent that has it.

  • Unowned agent detection: pause it, or approve it with an owner and limits

Models

The layer under the agents, with a pause that reaches Outpost

Models lists the AI models Kestrel's agents run on, with the same verifiable risk facts as the AI map, the data seen in each model's agent traffic, and the agents that depend on it. A person can pause an approved model, and every agent on it stops at once, in Umbra and in Outpost, where the bots fly home and their work goes back to the Inbox for the rest of the crew. Resuming the model frees exactly those bots. It answers the question a CIO asks the day a vendor has an incident: which of our agents break if we pull this model?

  • Only approved models can carry agents; unapproved ones are governed by the rules
  • Umbra lists its own drafting model here too, with its real test results
Corona and the policy replay

Write a rule in plain English, then test it on last month

Corona turns a sentence into a rule, points out anything it was unsure about, and restates the rule in English so a reviewer can check it. The draft comes from a real model (gpt-oss-120b on Groq), called through a small server of mine that holds the key; a checker drops anything outside Umbra's rule language, and if the model is down, Umbra drafts with its own rules-based parser and says so. Before the rule is turned on, "Test on last month" re-runs the last 30 days against it and shows how many decisions would change, and for which people and teams.

Incidents

Fewer alerts, clearer decisions

Umbra groups related events into one case per decision someone has to make. Cases the rules already handled are closed automatically, with the evidence kept. Corona writes a short summary for the analyst and a friendlier note for the employee involved.

Demand Map

Where your company wants to use AI

Unapproved usage is grouped by team and task, then scored by how often it happens, how much of it runs on unapproved tools, and whether it is growing. The result is a now, next and later list of where to roll out approved AI. This is the part that turns a security tool into a planning tool.

Assurance

Audit evidence on demand

Eight controls are measured from what the product is actually doing and mapped to the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF and SOC 2. You can export an evidence pack, or a full inventory of AI models, services and agents in the CycloneDX format. A five-level maturity scale gives the board a simple way to track progress.

Employee view

What employees actually see

Sensitive details are masked before sending, there is a one-click switch to the approved tool, and when nothing is wrong Umbra stays out of the way. The check runs on the person's own device before anything is sent.

Corona's rule drafting is the only place a model works inside Umbra, so it is the one place I had to prove it helps. What it showed

The model earns its place on the way people actually write: paraphrases and team names, where the parser fails. On plain wording the two are close.

Where it is weaker

When a sentence hides an instruction, the model handled 2 of 4. In the other two it refused the whole sentence instead of drafting the genuine half: safe, but not useful. It never drafted an unsafe rule (0 of 51), and the checker refuses allow-everything rules anyway. Getting the genuine half out is the next thing to improve.

Why this model

I tested two free options. Groq returned a checked draft in a median of 481 ms (90th percentile 609 ms) across all 51 test sentences, timed end to end through the live server on 26 October 2026; a Gemini model answered correctly but several times slower, and its free daily quota ran out partway through the test. For a drafting box that a person waits on, speed decided it.

Model card

Modelgpt-oss-120b, OpenAI's open-weight model, served by Groq. The same model runs CardRight's merchant reader and Conatus's planner.
JobTurn one policy sentence into a draft rule in Umbra's rule language. Nothing else. It never sees live traffic and never makes an enforcement decision.
SettingsTemperature 0, JSON output only, low reasoning effort. Every answer is checked against the rule language before anyone sees it, and anything that does not fit is dropped and named as a doubt.
Why this oneThree reasons. It returned drafts in a median of 481 ms, fast enough for a box a person waits on. It beat the rules-based parser by 15 drafts on the same 51 sentences. And its weights are open, so a regulated customer could run it inside their own cloud and keep policy text away from a third party, which matters to the buyer Umbra is built for.
Tested againstThe rules-based parser (28 of 51) and a Gemini Flash model, which drafted correctly but answered several times slower and ran out of free daily quota during the test.

08  When it goes wrong

Every failure is designed to fail closed.

A governance product earns trust in its bad moments. These are the four that matter, what Umbra does in each, and the evidence behind it.

WhenWhat Umbra doesEvidence
The model drafts the wrong ruleThe usual miss in testing was a dropped condition. "Block source code from tools that train on inputs" came back as "block everything sent to tools that train on inputs". A dropped condition makes a rule broader, so it blocks too much rather than letting data out. Every draft is shown in plain words, replayed on past traffic so its effect is visible, and switched on only by a person.8 misses in 51, 0 unsafe drafts.

12  Where Umbra stops

Umbra decides which AI gets in. It does not run the work.

Umbra is a governance product. Its user is the security or risk lead, its timescale is weeks and quarters, and what it looks at is every AI tool across the company and the content flowing into it. Once an agent is approved, with a named owner and a list of what it may touch, Umbra's part is done.

What happens next is a different job for a different person: someone has to run that agent day to day, give a yes or no on its risky actions and answer for its output. I built that as a separate product, Outpost. It takes exactly what Umbra hands over, an owner and a list of what the agent may touch, and nothing else. The two share a fictional company, Kestrel Mutual, and that single handoff.

In the demo you can do both ends yourself. Umbra finds Agent 7f3, a Marketing agent with no owner that drafts emails to customers and also posts files to the web with confidential data in them. "Review Agent 7f3" offers two equal choices: pause it, or approve it with limits. Approving opens a panel where a person picks the owner and ticks its permissions, which become its badge in Outpost. Sending email and reading contacts start ticked; sending data outside the company starts unticked, with the reason shown. "Approve and send to Outpost" hands it over as Fern. Before deciding, the red-team drill on the Overview runs three hostile sample cases through the same rules as real traffic: the agent's post to an outside host is blocked by rule P-07, and the other two get through and show in red, each with the switched-off rule that would catch it.
