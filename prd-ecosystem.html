Product requirements document · Ecosystem feature · Umbra and Outpost

Governed agents, from approval to audit.

The feature that joins two separate products into one system of AI governance: an agent approved in Umbra runs in Outpost within the limits Umbra set, and every decision in both lands in one trail a regulator can follow.

ProductsUmbra and OutpostTypeEcosystem feature PRD
AuthorMurtuza, product managerVersion1.0, 16 November 2026
StatusPartly built in the demoParentsUmbra v1 · Outpost v1

An example PRD. Both products are concept products with no customers, Kestrel Mutual is made up, and regulatory points are context for product decisions, not legal advice.

01  Summary

One chain of accountability, from the door to every action.

Umbra decides which AI gets in. Outpost runs the agents that got in. Each works alone, but a regulator or auditor asks one question that spans both: who allowed this agent, on which model, to do what, and who said yes to each risky thing it did?

This feature joins the two products into one governance system. An agent approved in Umbra arrives in Outpost carrying its owner, approved model, permissions and money limit. A model paused in Umbra stops every bot that runs on it. And every decision in both products links into one audit trail that can be exported as evidence.

Some of this already works in the demo: docking, rejection of incomplete handoffs and the model pause. This PRD defines the full contract, and the audit trail that makes it worth something to a regulated buyer.

02  Problem

Governance breaks at the handoff.

Where it breaks todayWhat goes wrong
Agents with no ownerAgents call other agents that call tools, and the chain can end with no single person responsible. In a 2026 Cloud Security Alliance survey, 53% said their agents had gone beyond their intended permissions.
Approval and operation in different placesThe team that approves an AI system rarely sees what it does next, and the team running it rarely knows what was approved.
A vendor incident with no blast radiusWhen a model provider has a problem, nobody can say which agents depend on it, or stop them in one move.
Evidence in piecesIn discovery, showing how something was approved meant collecting emails, documents and policy versions from several systems. Agent actions add a second, separate log.

Survey figure as cited in the Outpost case study (the study was commissioned by Zenity). Discovery findings are my summaries of ten conversations across both products.

03  Why regulation makes this urgent

Regulators will ask for a record, not a policy.

The EU AI Act's transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026, and the 2026 Digital Omnibus moved its high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027: delayed, not dropped. Standards such as ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI RMF ask the same thing in different words: show who is accountable for each AI system, what it is allowed to do, and how a person stays in control.

Companies already answer this for people with a delegation of authority: who may sign off what, up to how much. This feature gives agents the same instrument, with Umbra setting the authority and Outpost enforcing it action by action.

What an auditor asksWhere the answer comes from
Which AI systems are in use, and who owns each?Umbra's inventory: every tool, model and agent, with a named owner
Who approved this agent, and on what facts?Umbra's approval record: decision, reviewer, model, permissions
What is it allowed to do alone?The authority table Outpost enforces: badge, money limit, locked rules
Did a person approve each risky action?Outpost's logbook: every hold, every yes, and the rule that fired
Can you stop it?The model pause and the bot pause, both recorded

Mapping to specific articles and controls is a requirement below (E9), to be reviewed with a compliance lead. This is not legal advice.

04  Goals and non-goals

Two products, one source of truth for each agent.

Goals

  1. No agent works in Outpost without an owner, an approved model and a written list of what it may touch.
  2. Umbra is the single source of truth for what an agent is allowed. Outpost never widens it.
  3. Pausing a model stops every dependent bot at once, and only Umbra can resume it.
  4. One exported trail answers an auditor's five questions for any agent.

Non-goals

  1. Outpost does not approve agents. Umbra does not run work.
  2. No model decides admission, permissions or any allow, hold or stop.
  3. Not a legal compliance product. It produces evidence; people judge compliance.
  4. No production agent gateway in this release. It stays deferred.

06  Flows

Admit, pause, revoke.

Admit

Approve with limits

A reviewer in Umbra names the owner, picks the approved model and sets the badge and limit. The agent docks in Outpost as Supervised. In the demo, Agent 7f3 arrives as Fern.

Pause

Stop a model

A person pauses a model in Umbra. Every bot on it stops in Outpost. Work others can do goes to the crew; work only those bots can do waits in the Inbox. Only Umbra can resume.

Revoke

Withdraw approval

If approval is withdrawn or the owner leaves without a successor, the bot leaves the crew, its open work returns to the Inbox, and both products record why.

09  Risks and open questions

Where the chain could still break.

RiskResponse
A real agent runtime calls tools around OutpostThe first real connection must prove every tool call passes the decision point. Until then, the claim is limited to the demo.
The two products disagree about an agentUmbra wins on what is allowed. Outpost wins on what happened. Neither overwrites the other.
Platform agent registries overlapMicrosoft Agent 365 covers agents built in Microsoft 365. This chain covers agents across vendors, plus earned trust.

Open questions

  1. Who owns an agent when its owner leaves and nobody is named?
  2. Which framework controls should the export map to first, for the first regulated customer?
  3. Do buyers want this as one product, or two products that connect?
