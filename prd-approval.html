Product requirements document · Feature · Example

AI tool approval, in days, not weeks.

A requested or discovered AI tool gets a decision in under five working days, with the vendor facts filled in, the right reviewers for its risk, and the audit evidence produced as a side effect.

ProductUmbraTypeFeature PRD
AuthorMurtuza, product managerVersion1.0, 15 November 2026
StatusDraft for reviewParentUmbra v1 PRD · RICE 4.1

This is an example PRD written for the case study. Umbra has no customers yet, Kestrel Mutual is a made-up insurer, and reviewers are roles, not people. The research it cites is the five discovery conversations described in the case study.

01  Summary

Discovery without approval only creates a backlog.

Umbra's scan finds the AI tools people use without approval. In the demo, 11 of the 15 tools Kestrel staff used had never been approved. Each one needs a decision, and today that decision is slow: in discovery, one newly found tool took about two weeks and three functions to resolve.

When approval is slow, people keep using the unapproved tool, or give up on AI and keep the manual process. Both hurt the metric Umbra exists to move: the share of AI work done on approved tools.

This feature turns every found or requested tool into a structured request with the vendor facts already filled in, routes it by risk to the right reviewers, and records the decision as audit evidence. An approval with limits becomes a draft policy that a person switches on.

02  Problem and evidence

The slow part is deciding, not finding.

Two of the five people I spoke with described inconsistent or slow reviews, and a third struggled to assemble approval evidence for an auditor. These are my summaries of the conversations, not quotes.

What I heardWhat it means for this feature
Teams submit review information in different shapes. Missing details go back and forth, and one pilot was delayed by it.The request must arrive complete. Vendor facts are filled in from Umbra's catalog, so the requester answers only what only they know.
A newly found tool took about two weeks and three functions to resolve.Route by risk. A low-risk tool should not wait for the same three reviewers as a high-risk one.
Showing an auditor how a tool was approved meant collecting the risk assessment, the approval email, vendor documents and the policy version, from a GRC platform and shared folders.The decision record is the evidence. Nothing is assembled by hand later.
One team's automation stalled because nobody could say what evidence would satisfy the review.Show the requester, up front, what their tier needs and how long it should take.

Still unknown: how often this happens across companies, and whether it has a budget and an owner. Open questions in section 10.

03  Goals and non-goals

What this must do, and what it must not.

Goals

  1. A decision on any requested or discovered tool in under 5 working days, median.
  2. Every request complete on first submission, with no back-and-forth for vendor facts.
  3. Every decision produces audit evidence automatically: who decided, on what facts, under which policy version.
  4. A rejection always names an approved alternative, or says why there is none, so the person is not left with nothing.

Non-goals

  1. Not a procurement or contract system. Purchasing stays where it is today.
  2. Not a full vendor security questionnaire. Umbra fills in the facts it can verify and links the rest.
  3. No model approves or rejects anything. People decide, and fixed rules route.
  4. Not a replacement for the customer's GRC platform. Evidence exports to it.

05  How it works

From a tool found to a rule switched on.

  1. A request starts. Either an employee asks for a tool, or Umbra's scan finds one in use and opens a request for the team using it.
  2. Umbra fills in the vendor facts. From the AI map's catalog: whether the vendor trains on inputs, how long it keeps them, which attestations it holds and where it processes data. The requester adds only the use case, the team and the kinds of data involved.
  3. Fixed rules set a risk tier. The tier comes from the vendor facts and the data classes, the same way every time. No model is involved.
  4. The tier decides who reviews. Low risk goes to one security reviewer. Higher tiers add GRC, and privacy or legal where regulated data is involved. Each tier has a target time the requester can see.
  5. Reviewers decide. Approve, approve with limits (which teams, which data), or reject with an approved alternative.
  6. Limits become a draft rule. Corona drafts the rule from the decision in plain English, the replay shows its effect on last month's traffic, and a person switches it on.
  7. The record is the evidence. The decision, the facts it rested on, the reviewers and the policy version are stored together and export as part of the evidence pack.
  8. Everyone sees the result. The requester is told the outcome. The AI map and the Demand Map update.
06  Risk tiers

Route by risk, not by habit.

TierWhen it appliesReviewersTarget
LowVendor does not train on inputs, has a current SOC 2 or ISO 27001, and the request involves no personal, financial or health dataOne security reviewer2 working days
MediumPersonal or confidential business data involved, or one vendor fact missing or unverifiedSecurity and GRC5 working days

The tier rules are my proposal for review. They are written so a security lead can read and change them, and the same request always lands in the same tier.

09  Where AI helps, and where it must not

AI drafts. People decide.

The model does

Draft the rule for an approval with limits, as Corona already does for written policies: checked against Umbra's rule language, shown in plain words, replayed on past traffic, and switched on only by a person. On the existing 51-sentence test set, drafts were right 46 times against 28 for rules alone, with 0 unsafe.

The model does not

Set the risk tier, choose reviewers, or approve and reject. Those are fixed rules and people, so the same request always gets the same route and an auditor can follow it. Vendor facts come from the catalog with sources, not from a model reading vendor documents.

10  Rollout, risks and open questions

Ship it small, measure it honestly.

Phase 1

One pilot team

P0 requirements only, behind a flag, with one design partner. Measure days to a decision against their past requests.

Phase 2

The whole pilot company

Add reminders, draft rules and Demand Map updates. Watch the guardrails weekly.

Phase 3

General release

When the median is under 5 days for two pilots and the reopened rate stays under 5%.

RiskHow I would handle it
The fast lane approves something riskyLow tier needs every vendor fact verified. Any missing fact moves the request up a tier. The reopened rate is a guardrail.
Reviewers ignore the queueVisible target times, reminders and escalation. The requester can see where the request is waiting.
Catalog facts go out of dateEach fact shows its source and date. R12 reopens approvals when a fact changes.
It duplicates the customer's GRC toolUmbra owns the AI-specific request and the evidence. It exports to the GRC platform rather than competing with it.

Open questions

  1. In a company of 500 people, who owns the final decision when security and the business disagree?
  2. Do pilot customers want requests in Umbra, or in the ticketing tool they already use? This decides whether R11 moves up.
  3. Which GRC platforms do the first customers use, so the evidence export fits them?
  4. How often do vendor facts change in practice? This decides whether R12 is worth building early.
